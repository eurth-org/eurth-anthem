# Glossary of anthem and hymn metre terms

Made by `npm run content` from `src/content/glossary.ts`: 62 terms.

## The song

What an anthem is made of.

- **National anthem** `#national-anthem`: A song that stands for a country, sung or played at its ceremonies. Most are hymns or marches in style, and most are short.
- **Hymn** `#hymn`: A song of praise or prayer, made to be sung by everyone present. Its verses all share one metre, so one tune serves them all.
- **March** `#march`: Music to walk in step to, with a firm beat in twos or fours. An anthem in march style sounds resolute where a hymn sounds grave.
- **Verse** (stanza) `#verse`: A group of lines that is sung once to the tune. Each verse says something new; the tune is the same every time.
- **Chorus** (refrain) `#chorus`: A verse that comes back after the others, with the same words each time. It is the part a crowd learns first, so it usually carries the country’s name.
- **Quatrain** `#quatrain`: A verse of four lines. Every verse Anthem writes is a quatrain.
- **Couplet** `#couplet`: Two lines that belong together. A four-line verse is two couplets: the first line of each opens a thought and the second answers it.
- **Line** `#line`: One row of a verse. In a hymn a line has a fixed number of syllables and ends where the singer breathes.
- **Lyrics** `#lyrics`: The words of a song, as opposed to its music.
- **Lyric sheet** (hymn sheet) `#lyric-sheet`: The words set out for singers: title, numbered verses, the chorus set apart, and the metre named so a tune can be chosen.
- **Short version** `#short-version`: The part of an anthem that is actually sung on most occasions, often one verse and the chorus. Many countries name it officially.

## Metre

How a line is measured, so that words fit a tune.

- **Syllable** `#syllable`: One beat of a word as it is spoken: “an-them” has two, “har-mo-ny” three. Hymn lines are counted in syllables.
- **Stress** (accent) `#stress`: The extra weight a syllable gets when a word is spoken: FREE-dom, be-LONG. Every word of two syllables or more has one main stress.
- **Beat** `#beat`: The pulse of a line. Strong and weak beats take turns, and a line sings well when its stressed syllables land on the strong ones.
- **Metre** (meter) `#metre`: The pattern a line of verse keeps: how many syllables it has, and where the strong beats fall.
- **Hymn metre** `#hymn-metre`: A verse’s metre written as the number of syllables in each line: 8.7.8.7 is four lines of eight, seven, eight and seven syllables. Any text can be sung to any tune with the same numbers and the same beat.
- **Foot** `#foot`: The unit a metre repeats: one stressed syllable with one or two unstressed ones, in a set order.
- **Iamb** `#iamb`: A foot of two syllables, weak then strong: a-RISE, the LAND. Lines built from iambs start softly and end firmly.
- **Trochee** `#trochee`: A foot of two syllables, strong then weak: HOME-land, VAL-ley. Lines built from trochees start with a stamp.
- **Dactyl** `#dactyl`: A foot of three syllables, strong then two weak: HAR-mo-ny. It gives a waltzing line. Anthem does not write in dactyls.
- **Anapaest** `#anapaest`: A foot of three syllables, two weak then strong: ev-er-MORE. It gives a galloping line. Anthem does not write in anapaests.
- **Iambic** `#iambic`: Made of iambs, so weak beat first. Common metre and long metre are iambic.
- **Trochaic** `#trochaic`: Made of trochees, so strong beat first. Most hymns in 8.7.8.7 are trochaic, and so are sevens.
- **Tetrameter** `#tetrameter`: A line of four feet: eight syllables in iambs or trochees.
- **Trimeter** `#trimeter`: A line of three feet: six syllables in iambs.
- **Catalectic** `#catalectic`: Said of a line that stops one syllable short of its last foot. A seven-syllable trochaic line is a catalectic tetrameter: it ends on a strong beat where an eighth, weak syllable would have been.
- **Common metre** (C.M., 8.6.8.6) `#common-metre`: Four iambic lines of eight, six, eight and six syllables. The metre of a great many hymns and ballads, and the easiest to find a tune for.
- **Ballad metre** `#ballad-metre`: Common metre worn loosely, as in folk ballads: the same four lines, but only the second and fourth rhyme, and an extra weak syllable is forgiven.
- **Long metre** (L.M., 8.8.8.8) `#long-metre`: Four iambic lines of eight syllables each. Anthem keeps it for one occasion.
- **Short metre** (S.M., 6.6.8.6) `#short-metre`: Four iambic lines of six, six, eight and six syllables. Not one of Anthem’s metres, but you will meet it in any hymn book.
- **8.7.8.7** `#8-7-8-7`: Four lines of eight, seven, eight and seven syllables, nearly always trochaic. The long lines end on a weak beat and the short ones on a strong beat, which gives the verse its swing.
- **Sevens** (7.7.7.7) `#sevens`: Four trochaic lines of seven syllables each. Every line starts and ends on a strong beat, so it sounds bright and plain.
- **Doubled metre** (D) `#doubled-metre`: A metre sung twice over to a tune of double length: 8.7.8.7 D is eight lines. Two of Anthem’s verses fill one verse of a doubled tune.
- **Free verse** `#free-verse`: Verse without one fixed metre. In Anthem it means that each verse may take a different shape, with lines of five to eleven syllables; the rhyme scheme still holds.
- **Scansion** (to scan) `#scansion`: Working out a line’s syllables and stresses. A line scans when it has the right number of syllables and its stresses fall on the strong beats.
- **Upbeat** (anacrusis, pick-up) `#upbeat`: A weak syllable sung before the first strong beat. Every iambic line begins with one; a trochaic line has none.

## Rhyme

How lines are tied together by their endings.

- **Rhyme** `#rhyme`: Two words rhyme when they sound the same from their last stressed vowel to the end, and differently before it: light and night.
- **True rhyme** (perfect rhyme) `#true-rhyme`: A rhyme that is exact: the stressed vowels and everything after them match. The only kind Anthem writes.
- **Near rhyme** (slant rhyme, half rhyme) `#near-rhyme`: A rhyme that almost matches: home and stone, meadow and shadow. Poets use it freely; Anthem leaves it to you, in a line you type yourself.
- **Identical rhyme** `#identical-rhyme`: A word “rhymed” with itself or with a word that sounds exactly the same, such as one and won. It reads as a repeat, so Anthem does not count it as a rhyme.
- **Masculine rhyme** (single rhyme) `#masculine-rhyme`: A rhyme on one stressed syllable at the end of the line: land and stand. Lines that end on a strong beat rhyme this way.
- **Feminine rhyme** (double rhyme) `#feminine-rhyme`: A rhyme on two syllables, strong then weak: waters and daughters. Lines that end on a weak beat need it, which is why the long lines of 8.7.8.7 are the hardest to write.
- **Mosaic rhyme** `#mosaic-rhyme`: A feminine rhyme made from more than one word: guide us and beside us.
- **Rhyme scheme** `#rhyme-scheme`: Which lines of a verse rhyme with which, written in letters: lines marked with the same letter rhyme.
- **ABAB** (alternate rhyme) `#abab`: The first line rhymes with the third and the second with the fourth. The fullest of the three schemes, and the hardest.
- **AABB** (couplets) `#aabb`: The first line rhymes with the second and the third with the fourth. It cannot be done in 8.7.8.7, where neighbouring lines end on different beats.
- **ABCB** (ballad rhyme) `#abcb`: Only the second and fourth lines rhyme. The loosest scheme, and the one that leaves most room for the words you want.
- **End word** `#end-word`: The last word of a line, the one that carries the rhyme. Anthem never ends two lines of one anthem on the same word.

## Words and tunes

What happens when the words meet music.

- **Hymn tune** `#hymn-tune`: A melody written for verses of one metre. A tune and a text are separate things: the same words can be moved to another tune of the same metre.
- **Tune name** `#tune-name`: The name a hymn tune goes by, apart from any words sung to it. Hymn books print it in capitals, with the metre beside it.
- **Metrical index** `#metrical-index`: The list at the back of a hymn book that sorts its tunes by metre. Look up your anthem’s metre there to find every tune that fits it.
- **Setting** (to set) `#setting`: Putting words to music, or the music they are put to. A poem is set to a tune.
- **Contrafactum** `#contrafactum`: New words written for a tune that already exists. Several national anthems are contrafacta of older songs.
- **Melisma** `#melisma`: Several notes sung on one syllable. It is how a singer stretches a short line over a longer phrase of music; hymns use it sparingly.
- **Unison** `#unison`: Everyone singing the same notes. Anthems are written to work in unison, without a choir.

## In the tool

Words that mean something particular in Anthem.

- **Theme** `#theme`: What an anthem is about: the sea, the harvest, the crown. Each theme you choose is heard in every verse.
- **Mood** `#mood`: How an anthem carries itself: a solemn hymn, a martial march, hopeful, or pastoral. A solemn hymn says thee and thy; the others say you and your.
- **Words to include** `#words-to-include`: Your nation’s own words: its capital, a river, a tree. Anthem works each into a line where its rhythm fits, and tells you which found no place.
- **Re-roll** `#re-roll`: Asking for one line to be written again. The new line keeps the metre, makes sense with its neighbours and rhymes where the scheme asks; if no other line can, the old one stays.
- **Lock** `#lock`: Marking a line to be kept. A locked line stays as it is when the rest is written again, and the new lines are written to rhyme with it.
- **Pronunciation dictionary** `#pronunciation-dictionary`: A list of words with their sounds and stresses. Anthem reads syllable counts and rhymes from one, so it can check the lines you type.
- **Profile file** `#profile-file`: The file that holds everything about your nation across Eurth’s tools. Anthem reads the country’s name and places from it and saves the anthem back into it.

## The dictionary

Syllable counts, stresses and rhymes come from the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary, a free dictionary of how English words are said, made by the Speech Group at Carnegie Mellon University. Anthem bundles a copy, unchanged apart from its packing. The dictionary describes North American speech, so now and then a British rhyme is missed.
